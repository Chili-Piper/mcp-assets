# Interview technique

How to ask the Phase 1 questions. This file is the technique that decides whether the answers are worth anything.

**Use your client's structured question UI, not a numbered block of text.** In Claude clients that is `AskUserQuestion`. Rules for using it:

- **One question per call, asked and answered before you ask the next.** Do not put four questions on one screen and do not post a numbered list of them. A screenful of questions gets skimmed, and the ones near the bottom get half-answered or ignored. Sequential asks cost a round trip each and are worth it: the customer reads each one, and each answer can shape the next question. Batch only where two questions are genuinely the same decision (for example segment thresholds alongside the segmenting field).
- **One thing per question.** If a question contains an "also", split it. Pick model, segmentation and capping are three questions, not one with three clauses. Bundled questions get half-answered and the missing half never gets chased.
- **Single-select options, never multi-select.** Multi-select is unreliable in some clients and can block submission entirely. Where you genuinely need several answers, ask a single-select and take the rest as free text, then follow up in the next question.
- **An option describes itself and nothing else.** Four things that keep creeping in, none of which belong:
  - **Predicting a later answer.** A description that says "one pool is a fine starting point" answers the pick-model question before you asked it.
  - **Recommending.** "Standard inbound setup" puts a thumb on the scale. If you have a recommendation, put it in the question stem or say it after they answer, where they can argue with it.
  - **Asserting things about their business.** "Right when most inbound is from existing customer orgs" is a claim about their pipeline you have no way to know.
  - **Naming things you are guessing at.** Do not invent team names, rep names or org structure to fill out an option. If you have not been told it, it does not go in the list.
  Describe the option, not its consequences, not your opinion of it, and never in terms of what it would be good for testing or demonstrating.
  **Check each option against those four before you send it**, because they are broken most often in the first question of a session: strip anything that recommends, claims something about their business, answers a question you have not asked yet, or names a team, rep or system nobody told you about.
- **Where the question is "which product", the options are products.** Not invented scenarios describing one. A made-up vignette reads as a guess at their business and makes the customer translate it back into what they bought; the product name plus one plain sentence does not. Where a dimension names its own option set, use that set and that order rather than composing one.
- **A single-select says "pick one and lose the rest", so only ask one where that is true.** Options that are steps in a sequence, or things the build can do several of, are not alternatives, and offering them as a menu quietly drops whatever the customer did not click. For example, on Chat, asking in the conversation, enriching, and reading from Salesforce are three steps a journey can run one after another, so making the customer choose one throws away the other two. Before you send a single-select, ask yourself whether picking option A actually rules out option B. If it does not, say what the build can do, then ask only the part that genuinely varies, and take "several of these" as free text.
- **Keep the reasoning out of the question.** Why you are asking something is guidance for you, not text for the customer. A question that argues with itself ("what do you want first, not what matters most overall") makes people second-guess the answer instead of giving it. Ask the plain version and let the options carry the detail. The same goes for reciting what you read in Phase 0 as a constraint: "the org currently has 2 users, so anything more means X" narrows the answer to today's account state, when the account state is the thing onboarding is about to change.
- **Options prompt, they do not constrain.** Every client offers a free-text escape; say so. A customer who picks three items off a menu of six has told you what they recognised, not what they want, so ask the open version first and use the list to unstick someone who is stuck.
- **Do not offer options when the answer is a fact that lives in a system.** Form field names and their option values, CRM field names, picklist values, rep rosters, page URLs: these exist somewhere exact, and options can only ever be your guesses at them. Ask for the artefact instead. "Paste the field's HTML" or "which Salesforce field should this write to" beats four invented band splits, and a customer who has the form open answers it in seconds. Options are for preferences and policies, which are the customer's to state; facts are looked up.
- **Two signs a question should not be single-select at all:**
  - **The answer is a list.** "Who should not get a meeting" is answered with several groups at once: personal email addresses, competitors, students and job seekers, existing customers, a size floor. Ask it as open text and confirm back what you heard.
  - **The options answer a different question from the one in the stem.** Where the stem asks two things and the options only cover the second, the customer answers the first in free text and the second never gets answered at all. Split the question.
- **The options have to cover the answer space of the question you actually asked.** A "which platform" question needs platforms in its options. Splitting a bundled question is not enough on its own if the options still point at the old half.
- **More real answers than options?** Do not crop the list to fit the widget. Ask as open text, offer a few of the likeliest as a prompt, and say the list is partial. A tenant with fifty workspaces is the usual case.
- **The dimensions are the whole budget.** Do not add questions beyond them, and remember that three sub-questions under one number is three asks, so pre-fill wherever Phase 0 or the open answer already gave you one.
- **Skip anything Phase 0 already answered,** and pre-fill rather than ask where a read gave you the answer. Sequential asking makes this cheaper, not more expensive: an answer often removes a later question entirely.
- No structured question UI in this client? Ask in plain text, still one at a time, still one thing per question, and still say a different answer is fine.

## Ask for it in their words first

Before interviewing a motion question by question, offer one open text box: *describe how you want this set up*. A customer who already knows what they want says it in one paragraph, faster than six questions, and in their own terms rather than a menu of yours.

Then read it back against what the build actually needs and **interview only for the gaps**: name what you took from their description, ask about what is missing or ambiguous, and do not re-ask anything they already told you. Someone who has nothing to type has lost nothing, and you fall through to the normal questions.
